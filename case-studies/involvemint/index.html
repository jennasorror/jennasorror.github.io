involveMINT · UX case study

Helping volunteers join projects and submit their impact with confidence.

I redesigned how volunteers join local sustainability projects and submit proof of impact. Research showed that uncertainty about progression and completion was getting in the way of the work volunteers came to do.

My role

UI/UX Designer · UX Researcher · Information Architect

Duration

2 months

Team

2 UX designers · 1 project manager

Participant feedback

Found the redesigned workflow more intuitive.

Self-reported feedback after testing.
In this storyVolunteer needsProject entryProgress & feedbackTestingOutcome & lesson
01Volunteer needs

“Am I done?” should not be the hardest question.

Interviews and contextual inquiry with 15 volunteers revealed a journey with unclear step hierarchy, unnecessary backtracking, and little submission feedback. Participants were unsure both how to begin and when they were finished. I conducted the research, mapped a revised flow, and designed the interface alongside another UX designer and a project manager.

“I would finish one step but wasn’t sure if I was done or if I needed to do something else.”

Participant #7 · Testing the original workflow
02Project entry

Get volunteers to the right project sooner.

I addressed the start of that journey first. Navigation made finding the correct project harder than necessary, so the revised flow introduced QR-code entry. Volunteers could reach the relevant project from their physical volunteering context without searching through multiple options.

03Progress & feedback

Make progress visible, and completion explicit.

Once volunteers reached a project, the interface needed to guide them through it. I introduced clearer step hierarchy and visual progress indicators, followed by confirmation after submission. These changes addressed the research finding that completing a step did not always feel like completing the task.

04Testing

What the testing showed.

I tested the redesigned workflow against the original to examine whether clearer entry, progression, and feedback helped. Reported aggregate completion rose from 60% originally to approximately 95% after redesign. Some tasks and criteria changed with the new features, so these totals are not a controlled like-for-like comparison.

05Outcome & lesson

Make completion feel complete.

Alongside the task results, all 15 participants reported a more intuitive experience. This supported the direction of the redesign, while the differing task sets limited the strength of the numerical comparison. The lesson was twofold: make progress and completion explicit, and keep evaluation criteria consistent when measuring an iteration’s effect.
